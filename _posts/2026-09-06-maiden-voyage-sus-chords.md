**Warning: this post is a draft — content may change and errors may remain.**

**TL;DR.** I sat down with two versions of Herbie Hancock's *Maiden Voyage* — the Aebersold play-along and Jim Snidero's etude *Voyage* — plus lesson notes from two teachers, and they seemed to disagree about the very first chord. They don't. F#-/B, B7sus, F# Dorian, B Mixolydian, "E pent / A pent" (E and A major pentatonic), and "B bebop" are all the same seven notes of E major, named from different roots, minus or plus a note. The chord has no third, so it never commits to major or minor, and that is the whole reason so many names fit it. The practical upshot: over a sus chord the natural fourth *is* the chord, so Mixolydian is the default and Lydian dominant is off the table — and a modal tune is really a chain of short keys, four bars each. (The read time covers the main post; the appendix is skippable.)

## Two books, one tune, an apparent contradiction

I've been working through Jim Snidero's *Jazz Conception* on alto, and the etude *Voyage* is written over the changes of *Maiden Voyage*: B7sus for four bars, D7sus for four, back and forth, then a bridge of C7sus and Bb-13, then home. Thirty-two bars, AABA, eight chord blocks, four bars each. (Concert pitch, if you're reading a piano part: D7sus, F7sus, Eb7sus, C#-13. Tempos, key signature, and the one chord the charts spell differently are in appendix E.) I wrote my own formula in the margin — *B7sus := B E A, one four flat-seven* — and felt good about it.

![Top of Snidero's Voyage etude, Eb book, with my pencil notes C7sus := C F Bb (1 4 b7) and B7sus := B E A (1 4 b7)]({{ site.baseurl }}assets/maiden_voyage_sus_chords/01-snidero-voyage-header-annotations.jpg)

*Snidero's Voyage, Eb book, with my margin formulas.*

Then I opened the Aebersold play-along (Volume 54) to the same tune, and the first chord was F#-/B. Not B anything: an F# minor chord with a B under it. Aebersold's boxed instruction tells you to memorize the four Dorian chord/scales. Snidero's appendix says the scale for a 7sus chord is Mixolydian. And across the top of the Aebersold page, in my handwriting from a lesson with George Michael — the same George I thanked in my [2019 post on improvisation](https://brando90.github.io/brandomiranda/2019/10/31/jazz-improvisation.html) — it says *E pent, A pent, B bebop.*

![First chord of the Aebersold Maiden Voyage chart, Eb part, F#-/B, with my pencil note 'or B7sus (E pent / A pent)']({{ site.baseurl }}assets/maiden_voyage_sus_chords/02-aebersold-first-chord-annotations.jpg)

*Aebersold Volume 54, Eb part: F#-/B and my pencil note.*

So: is the note that matters an E (Snidero's suspended fourth) or an F# (the letter on Aebersold's chord)? Is the scale Dorian, Mixolydian, or a pentatonic? And in the back of my head, a rule from another teacher years ago: Lydian dominant is the *default* for dominant chords; Mixolydian is only for the real V7, the one with a gravitational pull toward a tonic. Was I supposed to be thinking Lydian here too?

I wanted this settled before I played another chorus over it. Here's where it landed.

## Notation, so nothing is ambiguous

Two conventions for the whole post. First, every note and chord symbol is in alto key (Eb): the charts I read are Eb parts, so the notes on the page are the notes I finger, and concert pitch is a major sixth lower (my B7sus is the piano's D7sus). The photos show whatever the page shows. Second, every pentatonic is labeled major or minor, and when I quote my pencil — *E pent, A pent* — those are major pentatonics. The reason is the key signature: Snidero writes *Voyage* with four sharps, which is E major, and E major is the parent scale the first block derives from. B Mixolydian is E major from its fifth degree, F# Dorian is E major from its second, and the pentatonics that fit the chord are the major pentatonics built inside E major on E and on A. Each major pentatonic has a relative-minor twin with the identical five notes (E major pentatonic is C# minor pentatonic; A major is F# minor), so I give the major name first, as the one built inside the parent key, and the twin in parentheses. The same logic names every block's parent scale: G major for D7sus, F major for C7sus, Ab major for Bb-13.

## What a slash chord actually says

A slash chord is a complete chord on the left and a single bass note on the right. F#-/B means "play an F# minor seventh chord and put a B in the bass." (The dash is Aebersold's minor sign; the seventh is implied — that's what "Dorian chord/scale" means.) When the bass note belongs to the upper chord, as in C/E, it's an inversion. When it doesn't — B is not in F# A C# E — the stack is a new chord, and the symbol is an instruction to the rhythm section: bass plays B, piano plays a plain F# minor seventh a fifth above it, roughly what Herbie's left hand does on the record.

Now count everything from B: B is the root, C# the ninth, E the fourth, F# the fifth, A the flat seventh. No D#, no D, no third of any kind. That is a B7sus with the ninth on top — B9sus4. The F# that looked like the important note is just the fifth; the third of B would be D#, and neither book has it. The E is the suspended fourth in both books. My margin formula was right as far as it went: the full chord is one, four, five, flat seven, with the ninth almost always added, and every one of those notes is inside F#-/B. The two symbols aren't two chords. Snidero names the sound from the bass; Aebersold names it from the upper structure.

There's a third common spelling, A/B — an A major triad over B, which gives root, flat seven, ninth, fourth with no fifth — and a rule worth keeping: for any X7sus, the minor seventh chord on its fifth over X, or the major triad on its flat seventh over X, both give you X9sus4.

## The chord has no third, and that is the whole point

Chord quality lives in the third. "Sus" replaces the third with the fourth. In classical harmony that's a suspension waiting to resolve down; in modal jazz it never resolves, and the unresolved, neither-major-nor-minor float is the entire mood of *Maiden Voyage*. Because nothing in the chord says major or minor, the seven-note scale that fits it can be named from any of its notes without being wrong:

- From B, it's B Mixolydian (Snidero).
- From F#, it's F# Dorian (Aebersold).
- From E, it's plain E major — the parent key.

Same notes: E F# G# A B C# D#, the E major parent scale from the notation note, entered from its fifth degree or its second. The mode name tells you which note you're treating as home; the chord symbol tells you what the bass plays; the notes don't change.

George's pentatonics are subsets of the same set, and both are major pentatonics inside E major. A major pentatonic — A B C# E F# — is the same five notes as F# minor pentatonic, and over B it's root, ninth, fourth, fifth, flat seven: every note a chord tone of B9sus4. It is also the F#-7 arpeggio plus the root, which is what Aebersold's box means by improvising over the chord tones. E major pentatonic — E F# G# B C# — is C# minor pentatonic, the brighter one, with the thirteenth and no flat seven. And why those two? Inside the scale there are three major pentatonics: on the root, on the fourth, and on the flat seventh. The one on the root (B major pentatonic: B C# D# F# G#) contains D#, the third — the note the chord exists to avoid. The ones on the fourth and on the flat seventh contain no third at all. My *one four flat-seven* formula and George's *E pent, A pent* (E and A major pentatonic) are the same fact from two sides: the two notes that define the sus are the roots of the two pentatonics that fit it. B bebop is the full scale plus one chromatic passing tone, A#, for eighth-note lines.

![The solo section of the Aebersold chart with the printed F# Dorian scale and my pencil notes B bebop, E pent, and (B sus) A pent]({{ site.baseurl }}assets/maiden_voyage_sus_chords/03-aebersold-solos-f-sharp-dorian-annotations.jpg)

*The printed F# Dorian scale under the solo section, with George's three suggestions in my hand.*

So the only place my three sources genuinely differ is a single note: D#. To Aebersold it's the sixth of F# Dorian, a color tone. To Snidero it's the third of B, in the scale but not a resting tone on a sus. To George's pentatonics it doesn't exist. Practical translation: pass through it, don't sit on it. Every voicing of this chord has an E in it, and a held D# against that E sounds like a mistake.

## Snidero's one confusing sentence

The appendix of *Jazz Conception* pairs each scale with a line from one of the etudes, transposed to C. It insists — I had underlined it — that you have to see how a line works *inside the tune*: most of these scales are modes of a parent key, and the lines are heard in relation to that key, in a progression that comes from somewhere and goes somewhere. The example is a ii–V–I in F from *Confirmed*: the C7 bar uses C Mixolydian, but you hear F major, because C Mixolydian *is* the F major scale and the progression resolves to F. Then the sentence I'd scrawled "what does this mean?" next to: modal tunes like *Voyage* are the exception; an inside line over them sounds as if it's in the key of the correlating scale.

![The paragraph in Snidero's appendix about modal tunes, underlined, with my margin note Q: what does this mean?]({{ site.baseurl }}assets/maiden_voyage_sus_chords/04-snidero-appendix-modal-tunes-sentence.jpg)

*The sentence in question, with my margin note.*

Here's what it means. In a functional tune the chord is a waypoint and the scale is a window onto the key — scale is not key. In a modal tune the chord doesn't go anywhere; it sits for four bars and is the entire harmonic world, so the correlating scale *is* the key. Over B7sus you are in the key of B Mixolydian, full stop, then you modulate to D Mixolydian for four bars. A modal tune is a chain of short keys. And with no third to anchor major or minor, which note sounds like "home" is decided by your line — that ambiguity is the tune, and it's why one book names the chord from B and the other from F#.

That reframing changed how I hear the form: the A section's only harmonic event is a minor-third shift from the E major parent scale to the G major one (the exact notes that move are in the map below), and the bridge is two fresh parent scales, F and then Ab.

## Lydian dominant, Mixolydian, and one question about the fourth

Now the older rule. It's a real rule about a real problem — but the problem doesn't exist here. On a dominant chord with a third, say B7 (B D# F# A), the tensions that sound good sit a whole step above a chord tone: C# above the root (the ninth), G# above the fifth (the thirteenth), and a whole step above the third D# is E#, the sharp eleventh. The natural fourth E sits a *half* step above the third: it rubs, and worse, it makes the chord sound suspended, dissolving the dominant quality. That's the classic avoid note. Lydian dominant (B C# D# E# F# G# A — F# melodic minor started on B, also called Mixolydian sharp four) replaces E with E#, so all seven notes become usable, which is why modern teaching calls it the default for a dominant chord standing on its own. The same principle puts Lydian first for major seventh chords on Snidero's scale page: on CΔ the natural fourth is a half step above the third E. One rule, two chord types.

![Snidero's scale reference page: C7 and C7sus both paired with C Mixolydian, C7#11 with C Lydian dominant, with my pencil note 1 4 b7]({{ site.baseurl }}assets/maiden_voyage_sus_chords/06-snidero-scale-page-c7sus-mixolydian.jpg)

*Snidero's scale page: Mixolydian for C7 and C7sus, Lydian dominant only for C7#11.*

When the V7 actually resolves, the story flips: the "avoid note" E is the tonic you're about to land on, and Mixolydian on B7 is literally the E major scale — the key-consistent choice, Snidero's comes-from-and-leads-to point again. The fourth is still not a note to sit on over the chord itself (in bebop it's a passing tone or the classic four-to-three resolution), but the pitch set is the key. Lydian dominant on a resolving V7 gives you E#, the raised tonic of the key you're heading to — chromatic color, not the home scale. A V7 resolving to a minor tonic usually gets darker scales: altered, Phrygian dominant (the fifth mode of harmonic minor, Snidero's "Spanish or Jewish" row), or half-whole diminished.

The single question that decides it: does anyone want the natural fourth? If the chord wants it (a sus), Mixolydian. If the key wants it (a V7 going home), Mixolydian. If nobody wants it (a dominant going nowhere, with a third to clash against), raise it — Lydian dominant. As a procedure, in order:

1. No third in the chord (any 7sus)? Mixolydian family, in every context, functional or modal. Never Lydian dominant, because sharp eleven contradicts the note the chord is built on.
2. The symbol says #11, or the melody has the raised fourth over the chord? Lydian dominant, obviously.
3. The chord resolves down a fifth to its major tonic? Mixolydian of the target key; altered notes and the sharp eleven are optional color.
4. The chord resolves to a minor tonic? Altered, Phrygian dominant, or half-whole diminished.
5. The chord goes anywhere else, or just sits — the IV7 in a blues, the backdoor bVII7, the tritone-sub bII7, the II7 in *Take the A Train*, any dominant vamp? Lydian dominant by default. (Bonus: bII7 Lydian dominant is the same notes as the real V7's altered scale — Db Lydian dominant equals G altered equals Ab melodic minor — so the two views agree on tritone subs too.)

A sus chord is the one case where the question never comes up. The fourth *is* the chord. Even when a sus chord functions — G7sus to G7 to C in a standard — the answer is still Mixolydian of the target key. So the default for B7sus is B Mixolydian in every context, which is why Snidero prints Mixolydian under C7sus without comment and reserves Lydian dominant for C7#11, the chord that asks for it.

## Every way to play over B7sus, inside to outside

Alto key; the notes-and-rationale version of each item is in the appendix.

1. **Chord tones and their arpeggios.** B, E, F#, A, C#, with G# as the thirteenth: F#-7, A, Amaj7, C#-7, the E major and F# minor triads. Rest on B, E, F#, A; pass through D#.
2. **B Mixolydian = E major = F# Dorian.** The official answer, under three names.
3. **The two pentatonics.** A major pentatonic (same notes as F# minor pentatonic) is home; E major pentatonic (same notes as C# minor pentatonic) is brighter, with the thirteen. B major pentatonic contains D#, so it's the least sus of the three.
4. **The no-third hexatonic**, B C# E F# G# A: the two pentatonics combined, or the E major and F# minor triads alternated. The purest sus color.
5. **Fourths.** The voicing itself, F# B E A C#, is three fourths and a third; the chain G# C# F# B E A yields quartal arpeggios. The McCoy Tyner and Herbie sound.
6. **B blues** (B D E F F# A) and B minor pentatonic: funky rather than floating.
7. **B Dorian** (the A major set): minor third, darker, inside because the chord has no third.
8. **B bebop**: Mixolydian plus A#; aim the downbeats at root, fourth, fifth, flat seven, ninth.
9. **Darker colors, a bar at a time**: Mixolydian flat six, Dorian flat two, Phrygian (the 7sus flat-nine sound).
10. **Not Lydian dominant.** The sharp eleven collides with the E the chord is built on.
11. **Outside devices**: side-slip a pentatonic a half step, anticipate the next block, play the other key on purpose and resolve.
12. **Rhythm, motif, space.** State a cell, repeat it, displace it, transpose it up a minor third with the chord — the composition's own device, and what Aebersold's box means by long lyrical melodies.

## The four-block map, and what I practice

For every block: the parent major scale, the two major pentatonics built inside it (same-notes minor twin in parentheses), and the bebop scale.

1. **F#-/B = B7sus** (bars 1–4, 9–12, 25–28): parent scale E major. A major pentatonic (F# minor pentatonic), E major pentatonic (C# minor pentatonic). B bebop. Rest on B, E, F#, A, C#.
2. **A-/D = D7sus** (bars 5–8, 13–16, 29–32): parent scale G major. C major pentatonic (A minor pentatonic), G major pentatonic (E minor pentatonic). D bebop. Rest on D, G, A, C, E. Everything from block one, up a minor third.
3. **G-/C = C7sus** (bars 17–20): parent scale F major. Bb major pentatonic (G minor pentatonic), F major pentatonic (D minor pentatonic). C bebop. Rest on C, F, G, Bb, D.
4. **Bb- = Bb-13** (bars 21–24): parent scale Ab major. Db major pentatonic (Bb minor pentatonic, the root pentatonic if you hear a minor chord), Ab major pentatonic (F minor pentatonic, the sus pentatonic if you hear Eb7sus), and Eb major pentatonic (C minor pentatonic) when I want the G, the thirteen in Snidero's symbol. Eb bebop, note-for-note the Bb Dorian bebop scale. Rest on Bb, Db, F, Ab.

George's rule in one sentence: over any X7sus, play the major pentatonic on the fourth of X and on the flat seventh of X, plus X bebop for runs. The notes that change between blocks: B7sus to D7sus, G#, C#, D# each drop a half step; D7sus to C7sus, B drops to Bb and F# to F; C7sus to Bb-13, A, D, E each drop a half step; the return to B7sus is the big reset, where only G#, C#, D# carry over.

In the practice room, in order: the major pentatonics on the flat seventh through a chorus on the slower Aebersold track (A, C, A, C, Bb, Ab, A, C), then the major pentatonics on the fourth (E, G, E, G, F, Db, E, G), four bars each; then the four Dorian and four bebop scales, landing chord tones on downbeats with each block's third as a passing tone; then the same material on Snidero's faster track; then his etude, noticing which of these shapes his lines use. And to close the loop on the pencil: "E pent" is E major pentatonic, the parent key's own pentatonic; E minor pentatonic (E G A B D) over B7sus is a darker, blues-leaning sound that leaves the parent key.

## The lesson

The two books were never in conflict. I was confusing a name with a note. The chord symbol names the bass, the mode name names the home note, the pentatonic name names a subset of the parent scale. Once you know the chord has no third, all the names collapse into one sound, and the only real decision left is how much to lean on D# — which is not much.

That is enough for a Sunday afternoon.

## Appendix

*Not counted in the read time above, and skippable. This is the raw material: the questions I actually asked, the answers, a summary of the takeaways, the twelve options in detail, the chart-by-chart details, and how to cite this post.*

### A. My questions, and the answers

1. **What does F#/B (Aebersold's F#-/B) mean?** A complete chord on the left, a single bass note on the right: an F# minor seventh chord (F# A C# E) over a B. Because B is not in the upper chord, the stack becomes a new chord — B9sus4.
2. **Isn't the note that differs E versus F#, and isn't F# the third?** No. Counting from B, F# is the fifth. The third of B is D#, and neither book has it. E is the suspended fourth in both.
3. **Why does Snidero write B7sus where Aebersold writes F#-/B?** Same notes. Snidero names the chord from the bass; Aebersold names it from the upper structure and tells the pianist what to voice. The chord's lack of a third is what lets both readings be true.
4. **What does "sound as if it's in the key of the correlating scale" mean?** In functional tunes the scale over a chord is a view of the key the progression is heading to (C Mixolydian in a ii–V–I in F is F major). In modal tunes nothing pulls on the chord, so its scale is the key for those bars.
5. **What are all the ways to improvise over B7sus?** The twelve options, inside to outside, are listed in the main post and detailed in section D below. Not Lydian dominant.
6. **George wrote E pent, A pent, B bebop; Snidero says Mixolydian; Aebersold says Dorian. Who's right?** All three. They name the same E major parent scale; the two major pentatonics (E and A) are its two third-free subsets; bebop adds one passing tone.
7. **Is "E pent" major or minor pentatonic?** Major. The key signature has four sharps, so E major is the parent scale of B Mixolydian and F# Dorian, and the pentatonics that fit a chord with no third are the major pentatonics built inside the parent scale on the fourth (E) and the flat seventh (A). E minor pentatonic (E G A B D) would leave the parent scale for a darker, blues-leaning color.
8. **Another teacher said Lydian dominant is the default on dominant chords and Mixolydian only for the real V7. How do I know which applies?** Ask whether the natural fourth is wanted. By the chord (sus) or by the key (a V7 going home): Mixolydian. By nobody: Lydian dominant. Resolving to a minor tonic: altered, Phrygian dominant, or half-whole diminished. A #11 in the symbol or the melody: Lydian dominant, obviously.
9. **What is the default chord-scale for B7sus?** B Mixolydian — equivalently F# Dorian or E major — in every context. Lydian dominant is excluded because a sharp eleven contradicts the sus fourth.

### B. Summary of takeaways

1. F#-/B and B7sus are one chord: F# minor seventh over a B bass is B9sus4 (B C# E F# A). F# is the fifth, E is the fourth, the third (D#) is absent in both books.
2. A slash chord is a full chord over a single bass note. Any X9sus4 can be spelled as the minor seventh on its fifth over X (F#-7/B) or the major triad on its flat seventh over X (A/B).
3. The sus formula is one, four, five, flat seven, with the ninth usually added. "Sus" means the fourth takes the third's place; in modal jazz it never resolves.
4. *Voyage* is *Maiden Voyage* up a major sixth: B7sus, D7sus, B7sus, D7sus, C7sus, Bb-13, B7sus, D7sus, four bars each. Aebersold's Eb chart: F#-/B, A-/D, G-/C, Bb- — the same notes. The Real Book spells the bridge's second chord from concert F# instead; still the same Ab major set.
5. Snidero's appendix: in a functional tune the chord is a waypoint and the scale is a window onto the key; in a modal tune the chord is the key for four bars. A modal tune is a chain of short keys.
6. One pitch set, many names: E major = B Mixolydian = F# Dorian; A major pentatonic = F# minor pentatonic; E major pentatonic = C# minor pentatonic; B bebop = the set plus A#. Mode name = home note; chord symbol = bass note; pentatonic name = subset.
7. The two pentatonics that fit a sus are the ones on its fourth and its flat seventh, because they are the diatonic major pentatonics that omit the third. The root pentatonic contains it.
8. D# is the only note the sources treat differently (Dorian sixth, passing third, or absent). Use it as a passing tone, never a resting tone.
9. The four-block map (alto): parent scales E, G, F, Ab major; major pentatonics A and E, C and G, Bb and F, Db and Ab (plus Eb for the thirteen); bebop scales B, D, C, Eb. The A section's only event is a minor-third shift where G#, C#, and D# each drop a half step.
10. The Lydian-dominant rule removes the avoid note (the natural fourth, a half step above the third) on dominant chords that have a third. Mixolydian belongs to the V7 that resolves, because its fourth is the target tonic. A sus chord has no third, so its fourth is a chord tone and Mixolydian is the default everywhere.
11. The single test: does the chord or the key want the natural fourth? Yes: Mixolydian. Neither: Lydian dominant.
12. The same avoid-note logic explains why Lydian is listed first for major seventh chords: on CΔ the natural fourth is a half step above the third E. One principle, two chord types.
13. Tempos: Aebersold plays it at quarter note = 96, Snidero at half note = 64 (quarter = 128). Build vocabulary on the slower track first.
14. Notation: everything in alto key (Eb); every pentatonic labeled major or minor with its same-notes twin; "pent" in my pencil means major pentatonic because the four-sharp key signature makes E major the parent scale from which the modes and the pentatonics are derived.

### D. The twelve options in detail

Alto key. Move everything up a minor third for D7sus, up a half step for C7sus, and use the Ab major set for Bb-13.

1. **Chord tones and upper-structure arpeggios.** The chord is B, E, F#, A, C#, with G# available as the thirteenth. Arpeggios made entirely of chord tones: F# minor seventh (the Aebersold symbol itself), A major triad, A major seventh (A C# E G# — flat seven, nine, four, thirteen, no third, and it sounds gorgeous), C# minor seventh (nine, four, thirteen, root), E major triad, F# minor triad. Rest on B, E, F#, A; pass through D#.
2. **B Mixolydian, which is E major, which is F# Dorian.** One set of seven notes, B C# D# E F# G# A, under three names. The book's official answer and the Aebersold-trained player's F# Dorian are the identical notes.
3. **The two pentatonics.** A major pentatonic (same notes as F# minor pentatonic): root, ninth, fourth, fifth, flat seven — every note a chord tone of B9sus4, the home sound. E major pentatonic (same notes as C# minor pentatonic): root, ninth, fourth, fifth, thirteen — brighter, no flat seven, very Herbie. The third pentatonic inside the scale, B major (G# minor), contains D#, so it's the least sus of the three.
4. **The no-third hexatonic.** B C# E F# G# A: the notes B Mixolydian and B Dorian share, which is the two pentatonics above combined, which is also the E major and F# minor triads alternated as a triad pair. The purest sus color available, because nothing in it says major or minor.
5. **Fourths.** The voicing itself is F# B E A C# — three perfect fourths and a major third, the *So What* voicing on B. The perfect-fourth chain inside E major is G# C# F# B E A, and any run of neighbors from that chain (B E A, F# B E, C# F# B) is a quartal arpeggio. The McCoy Tyner and Herbie sound.
6. **B blues scale** (B D E F F# A). It already contains the fourth and the flat seven, so it sits on the sus naturally; D and F are the blue notes. B minor pentatonic (B D E F# A) is the same idea, the "minor" reading of a chord with no third. Funky rather than floating.
7. **B Dorian** (the A major set: B C# D E F# G# A). Minor third instead of major; inside because the chord has no third; darker; and it shares the F# minor and C# minor pentatonics with Mixolydian, which is why those two pentatonics never sound wrong.
8. **B bebop** (B Mixolydian plus A# as a passing tone) for eighth-note lines that land chord tones on the beat. Snidero files the bebop scale under C7, not C7sus, because its alignment targets the third; over the sus, aim the downbeats at root, fourth, fifth, flat seven, and ninth, and add chromatic passing tones wherever the line needs them.
9. **Darker colors, a bar at a time.** B Mixolydian flat six (E melodic minor from B, adds G natural for a flat thirteen); B Dorian flat two (A melodic minor from B, a flat nine with a natural thirteen); B Phrygian (the G major set — flat nine, flat three, flat thirteen, the 7sus flat-nine sound). These rub against any G# or C# in the piano voicing, so they are tension, not home.
10. **Not Lydian dominant.** B7#11 raises the fourth to E#, which collides with the E the chord is built on.
11. **Outside devices for a static chord.** Side-slip a pentatonic a half step (G minor pentatonic for a beat, back to F# minor); anticipate the coming D7sus set in the last bar of the block; play the other key on purpose and resolve.
12. **Rhythm, motif, space.** The four-bar block is the phrase. State a short cell, repeat it, displace it rhythmically, then transpose it up a minor third when the chord moves — which is the composition's own device. Aebersold's box says the same thing in plainer words: long lyrical melodies, repetition, phrases you could sing.

### E. What the two charts actually say, and one caveat

Snidero writes *Voyage* at half note = 64, Latin feel (quarter = 128), in four sharps — the etude is literally in E major, and accidentals mark the changes: naturals in the D7sus bars, flats in the Bb-13 bars. Aebersold's Eb part runs three choruses at quarter = 96, spells the eight blocks F#-/B, A-/D, F#-/B, A-/D, G-/C, Bb-, F#-/B, A-/D, and prints the four Dorian scales under the solo section: F#, A, G, and Bb Dorian.

Seven of the eight blocks are note-for-note identical between the books. The bridge's second chord is where charts vary: Snidero writes Bb-13, Aebersold plain Bb-, and the Real Book spells it from the fifth (concert C#-7/F#, in Eb Bb-7/Eb, an Eb9sus4 — the reading I'd penciled onto the Aebersold page as *Bb-/Eb, Eb sus*). All three live in the Ab major parent scale, since Bb Dorian and Eb Mixolydian are the same seven notes; only the home note differs.

One honest caveat: Snidero's page lists Mixolydian for plain C7 too, the bebop-era, key-centered view; the Lydian-dominant default is the modern, post-Russell view. Neither is wrong: lines that run through a key favor Snidero's habit, standalone dominants and vamps favor Lydian dominant.
