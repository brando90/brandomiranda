*Brando Miranda — September 2026 · ~8 min read*

**TL;DR.** I sat down with two versions of Herbie Hancock's *Maiden Voyage* — the Aebersold play-along and Jim Snidero's etude *Voyage* — plus lesson notes from two teachers, and they seemed to disagree about the very first chord. They don't. F#-/B, B7sus, F# Dorian, B Mixolydian, "E pent / A pent," and "B bebop" are the same seven notes of E major named from different roots. The chord has no third, so it never commits to major or minor, and that is why so many names fit it. The upshot: over a sus chord the natural fourth *is* the chord, so Mixolydian is the default and Lydian dominant is off the table — and a modal tune is a chain of short keys, four bars each. (The appendix adds about four minutes and is skippable.)

---

## Two books, one tune, an apparent contradiction

I've been working through Jim Snidero's *Jazz Conception* on alto, and the etude *Voyage* runs over the changes of *Maiden Voyage*: B7sus for four bars, D7sus for four, back and forth, then a bridge of C7sus and Bb-13, then home. Thirty-two bars, AABA, eight blocks of four. (Concert pitch, if you're on a piano part: D7sus, F7sus, Eb7sus, C#-13.) I wrote my own formula in the margin — *B7sus := B E A, one four flat-seven* — and felt good about it.

![Top of Snidero's Voyage etude, Eb book, with my pencil notes C7sus := C F Bb (1 4 b7) and B7sus := B E A (1 4 b7)]({{ site.baseurl }}assets/maiden_voyage_sus_chords/01-snidero-voyage-header-annotations.jpg)

*Snidero's Voyage, Eb book, with my margin formulas.*

Then I opened the Aebersold play-along (Volume 54) to the same tune, and the first chord was F#-/B. Not B anything: an F# minor chord with a B under it. Aebersold's box says to memorize the four Dorian chord/scales. Snidero's appendix says the scale for a 7sus is Mixolydian. And across the top of the Aebersold page, in my handwriting from a lesson with George Michael — the same George I thanked in my [2019 post on improvisation](https://brando90.github.io/brandomiranda/2019/10/31/jazz-improvisation.html) — it says *E pent, A pent, B bebop.*

![First chord of the Aebersold Maiden Voyage chart, Eb part, F#-/B, with my pencil note 'or B7sus (E pent / A pent)']({{ site.baseurl }}assets/maiden_voyage_sus_chords/02-aebersold-first-chord-annotations.jpg)

*Aebersold Volume 54, Eb part: F#-/B and my pencil note.*

So: is the note that matters an E or an F#? Is the scale Dorian, Mixolydian, or a pentatonic? And in the back of my head, a rule from another teacher years ago: Lydian dominant is the *default* on dominant chords, Mixolydian only for the real V7. Was I supposed to be thinking Lydian here too?

## Notation, so nothing is ambiguous

Two conventions: everything is in alto key (Eb), since the charts I read are Eb parts, so concert pitch is a major sixth lower; and every pentatonic is named major or minor, so my pencil's *E pent* and *A pent* are major pentatonics — the four-sharp key signature makes E major the parent scale they are built inside.

## What a slash chord actually says

A slash chord is a complete chord on the left and a single bass note on the right. F#-/B means play an F# minor seventh and put a B underneath. When the bass note belongs to the chord above it, as in C/E, it's an inversion. When it doesn't — B is not in F# A C# E — the stack is a new chord, and the symbol is an instruction to the rhythm section: bass on B, piano on a plain F# minor seventh above it, roughly Herbie's left hand on the record.

Now count from B: root B, ninth C#, fourth E, fifth F#, flat seventh A. No D#, no third of any kind. That is a B7sus with the ninth on top — B9sus4. The F# that looked important is just the fifth; the third of B would be D#, and neither book has it. My margin formula was right as far as it went — one, four, five, flat seven, plus the ninth — and every one of those notes is already inside F#-/B. Snidero names the sound from the bass; Aebersold names it from the upper structure.

## The chord has no third, and that is the whole point

Chord quality lives in the third. *Sus* replaces the third with the fourth. In classical harmony that's a suspension waiting to resolve downward; in modal jazz it never resolves, and that neither-major-nor-minor float is the entire mood of *Maiden Voyage*. Because nothing in the chord says major or minor, the seven-note scale that fits it can be named from any of its notes without being wrong:

- From B, it's B Mixolydian (Snidero).
- From F#, it's F# Dorian (Aebersold).
- From E, it's plain E major — the parent key.

Same notes either way: E F# G# A B C# D#. The mode name tells you which note you're treating as home, the chord symbol tells you what the bass plays, and the notes don't change.

George's pentatonics are subsets of that same set. A major pentatonic — A B C# E F# — is the same five notes as F# minor pentatonic, and over B it gives root, ninth, fourth, fifth, flat seven: every note a chord tone. E major pentatonic is the brighter one, with the thirteenth and no flat seven. Why those two? The scale holds three major pentatonics, on the root, the fourth, and the flat seventh; the one on the root contains D#, the third the chord exists to avoid, and the other two contain no third at all. So my *one four flat-seven* and George's *E pent, A pent* are the same fact from two sides: the notes that define the sus are the roots of the pentatonics that fit it. B bebop is that scale plus one chromatic passing tone, A#.

![The solo section of the Aebersold chart with the printed F# Dorian scale and my pencil notes B bebop, E pent, and (B sus) A pent]({{ site.baseurl }}assets/maiden_voyage_sus_chords/03-aebersold-solos-f-sharp-dorian-annotations.jpg)

*The printed F# Dorian scale under the solo section, with George's three suggestions in my hand.*

Which leaves exactly one note my three sources treat differently: D#. To Aebersold it's the sixth of F# Dorian, a color tone; to Snidero the third of B, in the scale but not a resting tone; to George's pentatonics it doesn't exist. Pass through it, don't sit on it — every voicing of this chord has an E in it, and a held D# against that E sounds like a mistake.

## The sentence that stopped me

The appendix of *Jazz Conception* pairs each scale with a line from one of the etudes, and it insists — I had underlined it — that you hear how a line works *inside the tune*, because most of these scales are modes of a parent key. The example is a ii–V–I in F, where the C7 bar uses C Mixolydian but you hear F major, because C Mixolydian *is* the F major scale. Then comes the line I'd scrawled "what does this mean?" beside: modal tunes like *Voyage* are the exception, and an inside line over them sounds as if it's in the key of the correlating scale.

![The paragraph in Snidero's appendix about modal tunes, underlined, with my margin note Q: what does this mean?]({{ site.baseurl }}assets/maiden_voyage_sus_chords/04-snidero-appendix-modal-tunes-sentence.jpg)

*The sentence I had to sit with, and my margin note.*

It took me a few readings, and the sentence is exactly right. In a functional tune the chord is a waypoint and the scale is a window onto the key. In a modal tune the chord doesn't go anywhere; it sits for four bars and *is* the entire harmonic world, so the correlating scale is the key. Over B7sus you are in the key of B Mixolydian, then you modulate to D Mixolydian for four bars. A modal tune is a chain of short keys.

That reframing changed how I hear the form: the A section's only harmonic event is a minor-third shift from the E major parent scale to the G major one, and the bridge is two fresh parent scales, F and then Ab.

## Lydian dominant, Mixolydian, and one question about the fourth

Now the older rule. It's a real rule about a real problem — the problem just doesn't exist here. On a dominant chord that *has* a third, say B7 (B D# F# A), the tensions that sound good sit a whole step above a chord tone. The natural fourth E sits a *half* step above the third: it rubs, and it makes the chord sound suspended, dissolving the dominant quality. That's the classic avoid note. Lydian dominant (B C# D# E# F# G# A) replaces E with E#, so all seven notes become usable — which is why modern teaching calls it the default for a dominant chord standing on its own.

![Snidero's scale reference page: C7 and C7sus both paired with C Mixolydian, C7#11 with C Lydian dominant, with my pencil note 1 4 b7]({{ site.baseurl }}assets/maiden_voyage_sus_chords/06-snidero-scale-page-c7sus-mixolydian.jpg)

*Snidero's scale page: Mixolydian for C7 and C7sus, Lydian dominant only for C7#11.*

When the V7 actually resolves, the story flips: that "avoid note" E is the tonic you're about to land on, and Mixolydian on B7 is literally the E major scale. Lydian dominant there would hand you E#, the raised tonic of the key you're heading to — color, not the home scale.

So the single question that decides it: does anyone want the natural fourth? If the chord wants it (a sus), Mixolydian. If the key wants it (a V7 going home), Mixolydian. If nobody wants it — a dominant going nowhere, with a third to clash against — raise it, and that's Lydian dominant. A sus chord is the one case where the question never comes up, because the fourth *is* the chord. So B7sus takes B Mixolydian in every context, which is why Snidero prints Mixolydian under C7sus and reserves Lydian dominant for C7#11.

One caveat worth naming: Snidero's page lists Mixolydian for plain C7 as well — the bebop-era, key-centered view, where the Lydian-dominant default is the modern one. Neither is wrong. Lines that run through a key favor the first habit; standalone dominants and vamps favor the second.

## The lesson

The two books were never in conflict. I was confusing a name with a note. The chord symbol names the bass, the mode name names the home note, the pentatonic name names a subset of the parent scale. Once you know the chord has no third, all the names collapse into one sound, and the only real decision left is how much to lean on D# — which is not much.

That is enough for a Sunday afternoon.

## Appendix

*Skippable, about three minutes: the practice map, and everything I can play over the chord.*

### A. The four-block map, and what I practice

For every block: the parent major scale, the two major pentatonics built inside it (same-notes minor twin in parentheses), and the bebop scale.

1. **F#-/B = B7sus** (bars 1–4, 9–12, 25–28): parent scale E major. A major pentatonic (F# minor), E major pentatonic (C# minor). B bebop. Rest on B, E, F#, A, C#.
2. **A-/D = D7sus** (bars 5–8, 13–16, 29–32): parent scale G major. C major pentatonic (A minor), G major pentatonic (E minor). D bebop. Everything from block one, up a minor third.
3. **G-/C = C7sus** (bars 17–20): parent scale F major. Bb major pentatonic (G minor), F major pentatonic (D minor). C bebop.
4. **Bb- = Bb-13** (bars 21–24): parent scale Ab major. Db major pentatonic (Bb minor), Ab major pentatonic (F minor), plus Eb major pentatonic (C minor) for the thirteenth. Eb bebop.

George's rule in one sentence: over any X7sus, play the major pentatonic on the fourth of X and on the flat seventh of X, plus X bebop for runs.

In the practice room: the flat-seven pentatonics through a chorus on the slower Aebersold track (A, C, A, C, Bb, Ab, A, C), then the pentatonics on the fourth (E, G, E, G, F, Db, E, G); then the Dorian and bebop scales, chord tones on downbeats; then all of it on Snidero's faster track, then his etude.

### B. Every way to play over B7sus, inside to outside

Alto key. Move it all up a minor third for D7sus, up a half step for C7sus, and use the Ab major set for Bb-13.

1. **Chord tones and their arpeggios.** B, E, F#, A, C#, with G# as the thirteenth: F#-7, A, Amaj7, C#-7, the E major and F# minor triads.
2. **B Mixolydian = E major = F# Dorian.** The official answer, under three names.
3. **The two pentatonics.** A major pentatonic (same notes as F# minor) is home; E major pentatonic (C# minor) is brighter. B major pentatonic contains D#, so it's the least sus of the three.
4. **The no-third hexatonic**, B C# E F# G# A: the two pentatonics combined, or the E major and F# minor triads alternated. The purest sus color.
5. **Fourths.** The voicing F# B E A C# is three fourths and a third; the chain G# C# F# B E A gives quartal arpeggios. The McCoy Tyner and Herbie sound.
6. **B blues** (B D E F F# A) and B minor pentatonic: funky rather than floating.
7. **B Dorian** (the A major set): darker, and inside, because the chord has no third.
8. **B bebop**: Mixolydian plus A#; aim downbeats at root, fourth, fifth, flat seven, ninth.
9. **Darker colors, a bar at a time**: Mixolydian flat six, Dorian flat two, Phrygian.
10. **Not Lydian dominant.** The sharp eleven collides with the E the chord is built on.
11. **Outside devices**: side-slip a pentatonic a half step, anticipate the next block, resolve back.
12. **Rhythm, motif, space.** State a cell, repeat it, displace it, transpose it up a minor third with the chord.

Two spellings worth keeping: for any X7sus, the minor seventh on its fifth over X (F#-7/B) or the major triad on its flat seventh over X (A/B) both give you X9sus4.
