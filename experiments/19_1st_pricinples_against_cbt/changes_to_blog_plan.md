## 1. What the new post should say

The question is: **what changes a learned response when understanding it has not changed it?**

The argument should proceed in this order:

1. Animals could learn from consequences before human-style verbal reasoning existed. Conserved vertebrate action-selection and learning circuits support the evolutionary motivation, but a sea slug does not possess the human striatum.
2. Distinguish two related aspects of learning: changing behavior through its consequences, and repetition that can make responding habitual. Brando did not claim these were two wholly independent systems.
3. Explain why a goal-directed action and an experimentally defined habit are different. A habit can persist after its outcome loses value; repetition or fluent performance alone does not establish that habit formation caused a therapeutic benefit.
4. Preserve the personal point that a felt change can matter more than an intellectual realization, without equating all feelings with reward, dopamine, or learning.
5. Add the executive-demand concern: a technique may be difficult to deploy while distressed, but acute-stress findings do not establish that CBT fails in anxiety or attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD).
6. Explain an experience-first therapeutic preference and show where it overlaps with CBT's exposure, behavioral experiments, and acceptance-based approaches. Change can mean acting differently while a feeling remains. Distinguish research on alliance and reactance from an unverified patient typology or a universal rule about intervention timing; avoid declaring cognition irrelevant or insisting that it must always follow experience.
7. Explain why evidence volume does not establish comparative superiority or component mechanisms; distinguish researcher allegiance and publication bias from unverified claims about reviewer or guideline-panel composition.
8. State a falsifiable comparative prediction and evidence that would weaken it, with independent investigators, preregistered outcomes, and matched expertise.

**Working thesis:** For entrenched avoidance and cue-triggered responses, changing consequences and repeatedly practicing alternative responses may contribute more to durable behavioral improvement than verbal restructuring alone, particularly when executive demands are high. This is Brando's hypothesis; the cited studies do not establish a universal ranking of learning mechanisms or therapies.

## 3. Editorial and scientific guardrails

- **Strong position, explicit hypothesis.** Keep the first-person disagreement and the evolutionary ordering. Do not dilute it into a generic overview, but do not turn a hypothesis into demonstrated clinical superiority.
- **Compare like with like.** Compare therapeutic procedures or components; a brain region cannot “beat CBT.”
- **Keep procedures and mechanisms separate.** Behavioral activation can help without explicit restructuring and can still produce cognitive change. Similar treatment outcomes do not identify a shared mechanism.
- **No three-part-brain cartoon.** Do not claim the old brain is categorically larger, stronger, or unaffected by stress, or that all prefrontal cortex appeared at once.
- **No single learning rule for all therapy.** Operant conditioning, habitual control, and threat extinction are related but not interchangeable. Reward prediction error is not a synonym for every emotional experience or expectancy change.
- **Preserve the best objections.** Instructions can affect learning; practiced cognitive skills may become easier to use; cognitive restructuring can be beneficial; and mindfulness is not established as universally superior.
- **Keep public prose clean.** Use inline scholarly links, not a bibliography, evidence ledger, internal-path link, or provenance appendix in the blog. This plan holds the verification detail instead.
- **Apply bias scrutiny symmetrically.** Researcher allegiance is not automatically causal bias, not peculiar to CBT, and not a census of reviewers or committee members. Missing publications do not identify reviewer rejection as their cause. Keep counterevidence and critics' disclosed commitments visible.
- **Clinical observations are not validated categories.** The acceptance, reactance, and alliance ideas have research behind them; the exact four-way patient classification and prevalence claims have not been verified. Noncooperation is not evidence of delusions. An alliance–outcome association does not directly test when to use cognitive restructuring, and mediation evidence is not automatically causal identification.
- **Protect source material.** Acknowledging Andrea and emailing her the finished link are authorized. Publishing her correspondence, either original raw conversation, or the later voice-note transcripts is not part of the request. The speaker in those transcripts was not independently authenticated; describe the clinical perspective without naming its source.

## 5. Statistical checks and unresolved source limitations

These are published results, not new experiments or recomputed estimates. Intervals are the authors' 95% confidence intervals, not bootstrap intervals generated for this blog. Their uncertainty does not cover translation from animal or laboratory learning to psychotherapy, or generalization across diagnoses.

- **Carpenter:** 41 trials in the review; 40 reported continuous target-disorder outcomes. Pooled Hedges' *g*, a standardized treatment contrast, is **0.56 [0.44, 0.69]**, reported **p < .0001** for the random-effects pooled benefit against zero. Completer-only analyses were larger than intention-to-treat analyses. The indexed abstract reports **2,843 participants**, while the accessible author manuscript reports **2,835**. That discrepancy remains unresolved after checking both versions; the blog does not quote either participant total.
- **Richards:** **440 randomized**; the primary modified intention-to-treat analysis had **364 participants with complete data** (175 behavioral activation; 189 CBT). At 12 months, the reported difference on the nine-item Patient Health Questionnaire was **0.1 [−1.3, 1.5] points**; the prespecified non-inferiority margin was **1.9 points**. The per-protocol analysis included **286 participants** and reported **0.0 [−1.5, 1.6]**. The reported **p = .89** is not the test establishing non-inferiority; the blog explains the interval/margin criterion instead. No new non-inferiority p-value was calculated.
- **Johnco:** **249 adults**, an intensive exposure session, and **one-week follow-up**. The accessible abstract reports a nonsignificant small anxiety contrast favoring behavioral experiments over restructuring plus exposure, but does not supply its numeric estimate, confidence interval, or exact p-value. These are not invented. It also reports greater expectancy change and shorter treatment duration in the behavioral-experiment condition; the authors suggest possible modest advantages to challenging predictions after exposure rather than before. The blog now includes those findings, not just the counterevidence. This comparison of treatment conditions does not isolate timing from every other procedural difference or establish that pre-exposure cognitive work is harmful or unnecessary.
- **Ciharova:** **45 studies, 3,382 participants**. Against care as usual, standardized benefits were **0.57 [0.08, 1.07]** for restructuring, **0.52 [0.34, 0.71]** for behavioral activation, and **0.44 [0.28, 0.60]** for their combination. These are not estimates of differences between the three active treatments. Exact p-values are not supplied in the checked abstract. No detected difference is not an equivalence finding.
- **Goldberg:** **142 non-overlapping samples, 12,005 participants** across the full review. The comparison with established evidence-based treatments had **28 comparisons** at post-treatment, standardized difference **−0.004 [−0.15, 0.14]**. At follow-up, **15 comparisons**, **0.09 [−0.14, 0.33]**. The checked results passages do not provide exact p-values for these pooled contrasts. The article's disorder-level results and heterogeneity limit a blanket equality claim.
- **Safren:** **86 randomized medication-treated adults**; the post-treatment ADHD rating-scale contrast was **−4.63 [−8.30, −0.96] points**, **p = .02**, from the reported baseline-adjusted treatment-effect test. The program included organization/planning, distractibility skills, and cognitive restructuring; it was not a pure habit-training intervention. The full text clarifies that its 12-month assessment was **nine months after treatment**, and long-term comparisons were complicated by further care for nonresponders. The revised blog does not repeat the old draft's strong follow-up claim.

The five sources added for the evidence-volume and bias section have their denominators, statistical limitations, access levels, and institutional-evidence gap documented in [the bias change note, Section 3](changes_due_to_bias.md#3-checked-sources-and-numerical-scope). The post now cites 30 distinct scholarly sources, including four added for the clinician-related material.

The remaining uncertainty in the main thesis is substantive, not a missing citation to be filled with a convenient animal result: the cited evidence does not show that consequence-based practice is generally more effective than verbal restructuring for the specified problems. A fair component comparison with durable behavioral outcomes is the proposed test.

### Later clinician-related additions: verification and limits

The later source notes prompted the subsection **The goal, the patient, and the timing** within the experience-first discussion. The raw transcripts remain local; the blog contains an original synthesis, not their publication.

- **Hayes:** The author manuscript explicitly places acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) among third-wave cognitive-behavioral approaches and defines psychological flexibility through present-moment contact and action serving valued ends. Acceptance is not an end in itself; it supports values-based action. The blog uses this conceptual account without claiming ACT is generally superior or that changing feelings is never a treatment goal.
- **Stockton:** **12 mediation studies**, published during **2006–2015**, met inclusion criteria. The indexed abstract reports results generally consistent with the psychological-flexibility model but limited methodological quality and a narrow set of studied processes. No pooled effect, interval, or exact p-value is supplied there. The blog reports a qualified process-evidence conclusion, not a numerical meta-analysis or a proven exclusive mechanism.
- **Beutler:** **13 controlled studies, 1,208 patients**, reported standardized difference **d = 0.79** in the reactance/directiveness comparison. The checked abstract does not provide its confidence interval, exact p-value, or complete pooling specification; the blog therefore gives the direction of the finding without the numeric estimate. It does not infer a four-type classification or a timing effect from this treatment-matching result.
- **Flückiger:** The review reports **295 independent studies** and **over 30,000 patients**. The face-to-face alliance–outcome association was **r = .278 [.256, .299], p < .0001**, using a three-level random-effects meta-analysis with restricted maximum-likelihood estimation. The interval and p-value concern the pooled association, not an intervention effect. The full-text causality discussion acknowledges that longitudinal prediction is not an experimental causal demonstration; it also reports analyses addressing baseline characteristics and earlier symptom improvement, so the evidence is not dismissed as a meaningless correlation.
- **Rupture repair, additional background:** [Eubanks, Muran & Safran, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/pst0000185), Crossref metadata and indexed abstract checked. The repair–outcome association was **r = .29 [.10, .47], p = .003**, across **11 studies, 1,314 patients**. A separate comparison of rupture-resolution training/supervision found **r = .11 [−.09, .30], p = .28**, across **6 studies, 276 trainees/supervisees**—not 276 patients. The abstract does not specify the full testing model. This paper was checked as background rather than adding another citation to the blog; its training result prevents treating an association with repair as proof that training reliably improves patient outcomes.

**Four-way typology:** The checked Hayes, Stockton, Beutler, Flückiger, and Eubanks material did not validate the exact four-category classification or the notes' claims about how common the categories are. A targeted Crossref search for “psychotherapy four types patients resistance reactance Nardone” returned related resistance/reactance and general psychotherapy entries, not a validation of that classification; a second query for the strategic-therapy formulation was rate-limited. This was a bounded search, not an exhaustive proof that no theoretical typology exists. The blog converts the distinction into clinical questions about know-how, fear, pushback, and participation. It omits the unsupported prevalence claims and does not infer a diagnosis from cooperation or its absence.

**Timing:** The checked alliance and reactance sources do not directly establish that early cognitive restructuring causes resistance. The Johnco exposure-sequencing trial already discussed in the post is a material counterexample to a universal prohibition on pre-exposure cognitive work. Observing a response and adapting the next intervention remains a labeled clinical judgment, not a demonstrated mechanism or an assertion that cognitive techniques are useless.
