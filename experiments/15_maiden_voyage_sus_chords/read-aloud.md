TL;DR. I sat down with two versions of Herbie Hancock's Maiden Voyage — the Aebersold play-along and Jim Snidero's etude Voyage — plus lesson notes from two teachers, and they seemed to disagree about the very first chord. They don't. F-sharp minor over B, B seven sus, F-sharp Dorian, B Mixolydian, "E pent / A pent" (E and A major pentatonic), and "B bebop" are all the same seven notes of E major, named from different roots, minus or plus a note. The chord has no third, so it never commits to major or minor, and that is the whole reason so many names fit it. The practical upshot: over a sus chord the natural fourth is the chord, so Mixolydian is the default and Lydian dominant is off the table — and a modal tune is really a chain of short keys, four bars each. Two books, one tune, an apparent contradiction

I've been working through Jim Snidero's Jazz Conception on alto, and the etude Voyage is written over the changes of Maiden Voyage: B seven sus for four bars, D seven sus for four, back and forth, then a bridge of C seven sus and B-flat minor thirteen, then home. Thirty-two bars, A A B A, eight chord blocks, four bars each. (Concert pitch, if you're reading a piano part: D seven sus, F seven sus, E-flat seven sus, C-sharp minor thirteen. Tempos, key signature, and the one chord the charts spell differently are in appendix E.) I wrote my own formula in the margin — B seven sus := B E A, one four flat-seven — and felt good about it.

Then I opened the Aebersold play-along (Volume 54) to the same tune, and the first chord was F-sharp minor over B. Not B anything: an F-sharp minor chord with a B under it. Aebersold's boxed instruction tells you to memorize the four Dorian chord-scales. Snidero's appendix says the scale for a seven sus chord is Mixolydian. And across the top of the Aebersold page, in my handwriting from a lesson with George Michael — the same George I thanked in my twenty nineteen post on improvisation — it says E pent, A pent, B bebop.

So: is the note that matters an E (Snidero's suspended fourth) or an F-sharp (the letter on Aebersold's chord)? Is the scale Dorian, Mixolydian, or a pentatonic? And in the back of my head, a rule from another teacher years ago: Lydian dominant is the default for dominant chords; Mixolydian is only for the real five chord, the one with a gravitational pull toward a tonic. Was I supposed to be thinking Lydian here too?


I wanted this settled before I played another chorus over it. Here's where it landed.
Notation, so nothing is ambiguous

Two conventions for the whole post. First, every note and chord symbol is in alto key (E-flat): the charts I read are E-flat parts, so the notes on the page are the notes I finger, and concert pitch is a major sixth lower (my B seven sus is the piano's D seven sus). The photos show whatever the page shows. Second, every pentatonic is labeled major or minor, and when I quote my pencil — E pent, A pent — those are major pentatonics. The reason is the key signature: Snidero writes Voyage with four sharps, which is E major, and E major is the parent scale the first block derives from. B Mixolydian is E major from its fifth degree, F-sharp Dorian is E major from its second, and the pentatonics that fit the chord are the major pentatonics built inside E major on E and on A. Each major pentatonic has a relative-minor twin with the identical five notes (E major pentatonic is C-sharp minor pentatonic; A major is F-sharp minor), so I give the major name first, as the one built inside the parent key, and the twin in parentheses. The same logic names every block's parent scale: G major for D seven sus, F major for C seven sus, A-flat major for B-flat minor thirteen.

What a slash chord actually says

A slash chord is a complete chord on the left and a single bass note on the right. F-sharp minor over B means "play an F-sharp minor seventh chord and put a B in the bass." (The dash is Aebersold's minor sign; the seventh is implied — that's what "Dorian chord-scale" means.) When the bass note belongs to the upper chord, as in C over E, it's an inversion. When it doesn't — B is not in F-sharp A C-sharp E — the stack is a new chord, and the symbol is an instruction to the rhythm section: bass plays B, piano plays a plain F-sharp minor seventh a fifth above it, roughly what Herbie's left hand does on the record.

Now count everything from B: B is the root, C-sharp the ninth, E the fourth, F-sharp the fifth, A the flat seventh. No D-sharp, no D, no third of any kind. That is a B seven sus with the ninth on top — B nine sus four. The F-sharp that looked like the important note is just the fifth; the third of B would be D-sharp, and neither book has it. The E is the suspended fourth in both books. My margin formula was right as far as it went: the full chord is one, four, five, flat seven, with the ninth almost always added, and every one of those notes is inside F-sharp minor over B. The two symbols aren't two chords. Snidero names the sound from the bass; Aebersold names it from the upper structure.

There's a third common spelling, A over B — an A major triad over B, which gives root, flat seven, ninth, fourth with no fifth — and a rule worth keeping: for any X seven sus, the minor seventh chord on its fifth over X, or the major triad on its flat seventh over X, both give you X nine sus four.

The chord has no third, and that is the whole point

Chord quality lives in the third. "Sus" replaces the third with the fourth. In classical harmony that's a suspension waiting to resolve down; in modal jazz it never resolves, and the unresolved, neither-major-nor-minor float is the entire mood of Maiden Voyage. Because nothing in the chord says major or minor, the seven-note scale that fits it can be named from any of its notes without being wrong:

- From B, it's B Mixolydian (Snidero).
- From F-sharp, it's F-sharp Dorian (Aebersold).
- From E, it's plain E major — the parent key.

Same notes: E F-sharp G-sharp A B C-sharp D-sharp, the E major parent scale from the notation note, entered from its fifth degree or its second. The mode name tells you which note you're treating as home; the chord symbol tells you what the bass plays; the notes don't change.

George's pentatonics are subsets of the same set, and both are major pentatonics inside E major. A major pentatonic — A B C-sharp E F-sharp — is the same five notes as F-sharp minor pentatonic, and over B it's root, ninth, fourth, fifth, flat seven: every note a chord tone of B nine sus four. It is also the F-sharp minor seven arpeggio plus the root, which is what Aebersold's box means by improvising over the chord tones. E major pentatonic — E F-sharp G-sharp B C-sharp — is C-sharp minor pentatonic, the brighter one, with the thirteenth and no flat seven. And why those two? Inside the scale there are three major pentatonics: on the root, on the fourth, and on the flat seventh. The one on the root (B major pentatonic: B C-sharp D-sharp F-sharp G-sharp) contains D-sharp, the third — the note the chord exists to avoid. The ones on the fourth and on the flat seventh contain no third at all. My one four flat-seven formula and George's E pent, A pent (E and A major pentatonic) are the same fact from two sides: the two notes that define the sus are the roots of the two pentatonics that fit it. B bebop is the full scale plus one chromatic passing tone, A-sharp, for eighth-note lines.

So the only place my three sources genuinely differ is a single note: D-sharp. To Aebersold it's the sixth of F-sharp Dorian, a color tone. To Snidero it's the third of B, in the scale but not a resting tone on a sus. To George's pentatonics it doesn't exist. Practical translation: pass through it, don't sit on it. Every voicing of this chord has an E in it, and a held D-sharp against that E sounds like a mistake.

Snidero's one confusing sentence

The appendix of Jazz Conception pairs each scale with a line from one of the etudes, transposed to C. It insists — I had underlined it — that you have to see how a line works inside the tune: most of these scales are modes of a parent key, and the lines are heard in relation to that key, in a progression that comes from somewhere and goes somewhere. The example is a two-five-one in F from Confirmed: the C seven bar uses C Mixolydian, but you hear F major, because C Mixolydian is the F major scale and the progression resolves to F. Then the sentence I'd scrawled "what does this mean?" next to: modal tunes like Voyage are the exception; an inside line over them sounds as if it's in the key of the correlating scale.

Here's what it means. In a functional tune the chord is a waypoint and the scale is a window onto the key — scale is not key. In a modal tune the chord doesn't go anywhere; it sits for four bars and is the entire harmonic world, so the correlating scale is the key. Over B seven sus you are in the key of B Mixolydian, full stop, then you modulate to D Mixolydian for four bars. A modal tune is a chain of short keys. And with no third to anchor major or minor, which note sounds like "home" is decided by your line — that ambiguity is the tune, and it's why one book names the chord from B and the other from F-sharp.

That reframing changed how I hear the form: the A section's only harmonic event is a minor-third shift from the E major parent scale to the G major one (the exact notes that move are in the map below), and the bridge is two fresh parent scales, F and then A-flat.

Lydian dominant, Mixolydian, and one question about the fourth

Now the older rule. It's a real rule about a real problem — but the problem doesn't exist here. On a dominant chord with a third, say B seven (B D-sharp F-sharp A), the tensions that sound good sit a whole step above a chord tone: C-sharp above the root (the ninth), G-sharp above the fifth (the thirteenth), and a whole step above the third D-sharp is E-sharp, the sharp eleventh. The natural fourth E sits a half step above the third: it rubs, and worse, it makes the chord sound suspended, dissolving the dominant quality. That's the classic avoid note. Lydian dominant (B C-sharp D-sharp E-sharp F-sharp G-sharp A — F-sharp melodic minor started on B, also called Mixolydian sharp four) replaces E with E-sharp, so all seven notes become usable, which is why modern teaching calls it the default for a dominant chord standing on its own. The same principle puts Lydian first for major seventh chords on Snidero's scale page: on C major seven the natural fourth is a half step above the third E. One rule, two chord types.

When the five chord actually resolves, the story flips: the "avoid note" E is the tonic you're about to land on, and Mixolydian on B seven is literally the E major scale — the key-consistent choice, Snidero's comes-from-and-leads-to point again. The fourth is still not a note to sit on over the chord itself (in bebop it's a passing tone or the classic four-to-three resolution), but the pitch set is the key. Lydian dominant on a resolving five chord gives you E-sharp, the raised tonic of the key you're heading to — chromatic color, not the home scale. A five chord resolving to a minor tonic usually gets darker scales: altered, Phrygian dominant (the fifth mode of harmonic minor, Snidero's "Spanish or Jewish" row), or half-whole diminished.

The single question that decides it: does anyone want the natural fourth? If the chord wants it (a sus), Mixolydian. If the key wants it (a five chord going home), Mixolydian. If nobody wants it (a dominant going nowhere, with a third to clash against), raise it — Lydian dominant. As a procedure, in order:

1. No third in the chord (any seven sus)? Mixolydian family, in every context, functional or modal. Never Lydian dominant, because sharp eleven contradicts the note the chord is built on.
2. The symbol says sharp eleven, or the melody has the raised fourth over the chord? Lydian dominant, obviously.
3. The chord resolves down a fifth to its major tonic? Mixolydian of the target key; altered notes and the sharp eleven are optional color.
4. The chord resolves to a minor tonic? Altered, Phrygian dominant, or half-whole diminished.
5. The chord goes anywhere else, or just sits — the four-seven in a blues, the backdoor flat-seven dominant, the tritone-sub flat-two dominant, the two-seven in Take the A Train, any dominant vamp? Lydian dominant by default. (Bonus: flat-two dominant Lydian dominant is the same notes as the real five chord's altered scale — D-flat Lydian dominant equals G altered equals A-flat melodic minor — so the two views agree on tritone subs too.)

A sus chord is the one case where the question never comes up. The fourth is the chord. Even when a sus chord functions — G seven sus to G seven to C in a standard — the answer is still Mixolydian of the target key. So the default for B seven sus is B Mixolydian in every context, which is why Snidero prints Mixolydian under C seven sus without comment and reserves Lydian dominant for C seven sharp eleven, the chord that asks for it.

Every way to play over B seven sus, inside to outside

Alto key; the notes-and-rationale version of each item is in the appendix.

1. Chord tones and their arpeggios. B, E, F-sharp, A, C-sharp, with G-sharp as the thirteenth: F-sharp minor seven, A, A major seven, C-sharp minor seven, the E major and F-sharp minor triads. Rest on B, E, F-sharp, A; pass through D-sharp.
2. B Mixolydian equals E major equals F-sharp Dorian. The official answer, under three names.
3. The two pentatonics. A major pentatonic (same notes as F-sharp minor pentatonic) is home; E major pentatonic (same notes as C-sharp minor pentatonic) is brighter, with the thirteen. B major pentatonic contains D-sharp, so it's the least sus of the three.
4. The no-third hexatonic, B C-sharp E F-sharp G-sharp A: the two pentatonics combined, or the E major and F-sharp minor triads alternated. The purest sus color.
5. Fourths. The voicing itself, F-sharp B E A C-sharp, is three fourths and a third; the chain G-sharp C-sharp F-sharp B E A yields quartal arpeggios. The McCoy Tyner and Herbie sound.
6. B blues (B D E F F-sharp A) and B minor pentatonic: funky rather than floating.
7. B Dorian (the A major set): minor third, darker, inside because the chord has no third.
8. B bebop: Mixolydian plus A-sharp; aim the downbeats at root, fourth, fifth, flat seven, ninth.
9. Darker colors, a bar at a time: Mixolydian flat six, Dorian flat two, Phrygian (the seven sus flat-nine sound).
10. Not Lydian dominant. The sharp eleven collides with the E the chord is built on.
11. Outside devices: side-slip a pentatonic a half step, anticipate the next block, play the other key on purpose and resolve.
12. Rhythm, motif, space. State a cell, repeat it, displace it, transpose it up a minor third with the chord — the composition's own device, and what Aebersold's box means by long lyrical melodies.

The four-block map, and what I practice

For every block: the parent major scale, the two major pentatonics built inside it (same-notes minor twin in parentheses), and the bebop scale.

1. F-sharp minor over B equals B seven sus (bars one to four, nine to twelve, twenty-five to twenty-eight): parent scale E major. A major pentatonic (F-sharp minor pentatonic), E major pentatonic (C-sharp minor pentatonic). B bebop. Rest on B, E, F-sharp, A, C-sharp.
2. A minor over D equals D seven sus (bars five to eight, thirteen to sixteen, twenty-nine to thirty-two): parent scale G major. C major pentatonic (A minor pentatonic), G major pentatonic (E minor pentatonic). D bebop. Rest on D, G, A, C, E. Everything from block one, up a minor third.
3. G minor over C equals C seven sus (bars seventeen to twenty): parent scale F major. B-flat major pentatonic (G minor pentatonic), F major pentatonic (D minor pentatonic). C bebop. Rest on C, F, G, B-flat, D.
4. B-flat minor equals B-flat minor thirteen (bars twenty-one to twenty-four): parent scale A-flat major. D-flat major pentatonic (B-flat minor pentatonic, the root pentatonic if you hear a minor chord), A-flat major pentatonic (F minor pentatonic, the sus pentatonic if you hear E-flat seven sus), and E-flat major pentatonic (C minor pentatonic) when I want the G, the thirteen in Snidero's symbol. E-flat bebop, note-for-note the B-flat Dorian bebop scale. Rest on B-flat, D-flat, F, A-flat.

George's rule in one sentence: over any X seven sus, play the major pentatonic on the fourth of X and on the flat seventh of X, plus X bebop for runs. The notes that change between blocks: B seven sus to D seven sus, G-sharp, C-sharp, D-sharp each drop a half step; D seven sus to C seven sus, B drops to B-flat and F-sharp to F; C seven sus to B-flat minor thirteen, A, D, E each drop a half step; the return to B seven sus is the big reset, where only G-sharp, C-sharp, D-sharp carry over.

In the practice room, in order: the major pentatonics on the flat seventh through a chorus on the slower Aebersold track (A, C, A, C, B-flat, A-flat, A, C), then the major pentatonics on the fourth (E, G, E, G, F, D-flat, E, G), four bars each; then the four Dorian and four bebop scales, landing chord tones on downbeats with each block's third as a passing tone; then the same material on Snidero's faster track; then his etude, noticing which of these shapes his lines use. And to close the loop on the pencil: "E pent" is E major pentatonic, the parent key's own pentatonic; E minor pentatonic (E G A B D) over B seven sus is a darker, blues-leaning sound that leaves the parent key.

The lesson

The two books were never in conflict. I was confusing a name with a note. The chord symbol names the bass, the mode name names the home note, the pentatonic name names a subset of the parent scale. Once you know the chord has no third, all the names collapse into one sound, and the only real decision left is how much to lean on D-sharp — which is not much.

That is enough for a Sunday afternoon.

A. My questions, and the answers

1. What does F-sharp over B (Aebersold's F-sharp minor over B) mean? A complete chord on the left, a single bass note on the right: an F-sharp minor seventh chord (F-sharp A C-sharp E) over a B. Because B is not in the upper chord, the stack becomes a new chord — B nine sus four.
2. Isn't the note that differs E versus F-sharp, and isn't F-sharp the third? No. Counting from B, F-sharp is the fifth. The third of B is D-sharp, and neither book has it. E is the suspended fourth in both.
3. Why does Snidero write B seven sus where Aebersold writes F-sharp minor over B? Same notes. Snidero names the chord from the bass; Aebersold names it from the upper structure and tells the pianist what to voice. The chord's lack of a third is what lets both readings be true.
4. What does "sound as if it's in the key of the correlating scale" mean? In functional tunes the scale over a chord is a view of the key the progression is heading to (C Mixolydian in a two-five-one in F is F major). In modal tunes nothing pulls on the chord, so its scale is the key for those bars.
5. What are all the ways to improvise over B seven sus? The twelve options, inside to outside, are listed in the main post and detailed in section D below. Not Lydian dominant.
6. George wrote E pent, A pent, B bebop; Snidero says Mixolydian; Aebersold says Dorian. Who's right? All three. They name the same E major parent scale; the two major pentatonics (E and A) are its two third-free subsets; bebop adds one passing tone.
7. Is "E pent" major or minor pentatonic? Major. The key signature has four sharps, so E major is the parent scale of B Mixolydian and F-sharp Dorian, and the pentatonics that fit a chord with no third are the major pentatonics built inside the parent scale on the fourth (E) and the flat seventh (A). E minor pentatonic (E G A B D) would leave the parent scale for a darker, blues-leaning color.
8. Another teacher said Lydian dominant is the default on dominant chords and Mixolydian only for the real five chord. How do I know which applies? Ask whether the natural fourth is wanted. By the chord (sus) or by the key (a five chord going home): Mixolydian. By nobody: Lydian dominant. Resolving to a minor tonic: altered, Phrygian dominant, or half-whole diminished. A sharp eleven in the symbol or the melody: Lydian dominant, obviously.
9. What is the default chord-scale for B seven sus? B Mixolydian — equivalently F-sharp Dorian or E major — in every context. Lydian dominant is excluded because a sharp eleven contradicts the sus fourth.

B. Summary of takeaways

1. F-sharp minor over B and B seven sus are one chord: F-sharp minor seventh over a B bass is B nine sus four (B C-sharp E F-sharp A). F-sharp is the fifth, E is the fourth, the third (D-sharp) is absent in both books.
2. A slash chord is a full chord over a single bass note. Any X nine sus four can be spelled as the minor seventh on its fifth over X (F-sharp minor seven over B) or the major triad on its flat seventh over X (A over B).
3. The sus formula is one, four, five, flat seven, with the ninth usually added. "Sus" means the fourth takes the third's place; in modal jazz it never resolves.
4. Voyage is Maiden Voyage up a major sixth: B seven sus, D seven sus, B seven sus, D seven sus, C seven sus, B-flat minor thirteen, B seven sus, D seven sus, four bars each. Aebersold's E-flat chart: F-sharp minor over B, A minor over D, G minor over C, B-flat minor — the same notes. The Real Book spells the bridge's second chord from concert F-sharp instead; still the same A-flat major set.
5. Snidero's appendix: in a functional tune the chord is a waypoint and the scale is a window onto the key; in a modal tune the chord is the key for four bars. A modal tune is a chain of short keys.
6. One pitch set, many names: E major equals B Mixolydian equals F-sharp Dorian; A major pentatonic equals F-sharp minor pentatonic; E major pentatonic equals C-sharp minor pentatonic; B bebop equals the set plus A-sharp. Mode name equals home note; chord symbol equals bass note; pentatonic name equals subset.
7. The two pentatonics that fit a sus are the ones on its fourth and its flat seventh, because they are the diatonic major pentatonics that omit the third. The root pentatonic contains it.
8. D-sharp is the only note the sources treat differently (Dorian sixth, passing third, or absent). Use it as a passing tone, never a resting tone.
9. The four-block map (alto): parent scales E, G, F, A-flat major; major pentatonics A and E, C and G, B-flat and F, D-flat and A-flat (plus E-flat for the thirteen); bebop scales B, D, C, E-flat. The A section's only event is a minor-third shift where G-sharp, C-sharp, and D-sharp each drop a half step.
10. The Lydian-dominant rule removes the avoid note (the natural fourth, a half step above the third) on dominant chords that have a third. Mixolydian belongs to the five chord that resolves, because its fourth is the target tonic. A sus chord has no third, so its fourth is a chord tone and Mixolydian is the default everywhere.
11. The single test: does the chord or the key want the natural fourth? Yes: Mixolydian. Neither: Lydian dominant.
12. The same avoid-note logic explains why Lydian is listed first for major seventh chords: on C major seven the natural fourth is a half step above the third E. One principle, two chord types.
13. Tempos: Aebersold plays it at quarter note equals ninety-six, Snidero at half note equals sixty-four (quarter equals one twenty-eight). Build vocabulary on the slower track first.
14. Notation: everything in alto key (E-flat); every pentatonic labeled major or minor with its same-notes twin; "pent" in my pencil means major pentatonic because the four-sharp key signature makes E major the parent scale from which the modes and the pentatonics are derived.
