*Brando Miranda — October 2026 · ~12 min read*

**TL;DR.** Cognitive behavioral therapy asks you to catch an anxious thought, weigh the evidence and talk yourself into a better one. That is executive-function work, and it is done by the prefrontal cortex. But anxiety is exactly the state in which the prefrontal cortex works worst. Even mild uncontrollable stress causes "a rapid and dramatic loss of prefrontal cognitive abilities" (Arnsten, 2009), which is the point Judson Brewer builds *Unwinding Anxiety* around: "both willpower and reasoning rely on the PFC, which at these critical moments has shut down and isn't available." So CBT hands you the tool at the moment the tool is broken. If you also have ADHD, as I do, executive function is already the weak link before anxiety shows up. That's why mindfulness and concentration practice, which don't ask you to out-argue your own brain, make much more sense to me, and probably meds too. CBT does have real evidence behind it, and I go through the strongest case against my view below. My refined claim is narrower: the *argue-with-your-thoughts* part of CBT is the part that doesn't make sense to me, and the parts of CBT that seem to work look a lot like practice, not persuasion.

---

## A conversation that started this

I was recently talking with a friend who used to work as a clinical psychologist and is now doing a psychology PhD. We found out we both don't really believe in CBT. Not "it never helps anyone," but "the mechanism, as it's usually explained, doesn't add up."

I've been chewing on why for years, since I found Judson Brewer's work during my time at MIT (I mention it on my [performance psychology page]({{ site.baseurl }}/peformance-psychology.html)). So here is my attempt to write the argument down carefully, steelman the other side, and see what survives.

## What CBT asks you to do

The classic CBT loop goes something like this. Something happens. You notice an automatic thought ("I'm going to fail this talk"). You write it down, label the distortion (catastrophizing, mind-reading, ...), look at the evidence for and against it, and replace it with a more balanced thought ("I've given talks before and they were fine"). The feeling is supposed to follow the thought.

Look at what that requires, mechanically:

- **Working memory**, to hold the thought, the evidence and the alternative at the same time.
- **Inhibition**, to stop the automatic thought from running the show while you evaluate it.
- **Shifting**, to move from "inside" the feeling to "looking at" it.

Those three are more or less the textbook definition of executive function (Miyake et al., 2000). And executive function lives, to a first approximation, in the prefrontal cortex (PFC).

So CBT is a top-down strategy: use the PFC to regulate the rest of the brain. Fine. Now the question is whether the PFC is available when you need it.

## Anxiety takes the PFC offline

This is the part I like from Brewer. In *Unwinding Anxiety* (2021) he puts it like this:

> "Overwhelmed by uncertainty and fear of the future, our PFCs—the rational thinking parts of our brains—go off-line."

and, a bit later, the line that I think is the whole argument in one sentence:

> "Too many times, I've seen my anxious clinic patients try to suppress or think themselves out of anxiety. ... Unfortunately, both willpower and reasoning rely on the PFC, which at these critical moments has shut down and isn't available."

In his TED talk he adds that the PFC is "the youngest part of our brain from an evolutionary perspective" and "the first part of our brain that goes offline when we get stressed out." When it goes offline, "we fall back into our old habits." That's why, as the book's own description says, anxiety habit loops are something "we can't think our way out of or use willpower to overcome." (He has since written about CBT directly: his view is that "CBT intervenes at the cognitive layer," the layer that goes offline first. To his credit, he also says CBT "deserves genuine respect" and that if it has worked for you, keep doing it.)

That isn't just a nice metaphor. It rests on a solid line of neuroscience, most associated with Amy Arnsten at Yale. Her 2009 review in *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* calls the PFC "the most evolved brain region" and "also the brain region that is most sensitive to the detrimental effects of stress exposure." The mechanism: uncontrollable stress floods the PFC with high levels of norepinephrine and dopamine (an inverted-U: a little helps, a lot hurts), which weakens PFC network firing and working memory, while strengthening the amygdala and the habit circuits of the striatum. In her 2015 review's words, it ends up "rapidly flipping the brain from reflective to reflexive control of behavior." Evolutionarily that makes sense: if a tiger shows up, you want fast reflexes, not a pros-and-cons list. (Fair caveat: a lot of the mechanistic data is from rats and monkeys, and "offline" is a popularization of "impaired," not an on/off switch.)

Cognitive psychology says something similar from the behavior side. Attentional Control Theory (Eysenck et al., 2007) proposes that anxiety impairs exactly the inhibition and shifting functions of executive control, so anxious people get more captured by threat and are worse at redirecting attention. One nuance I find telling: anxiety hurts *efficiency* more than *effectiveness*, because anxious people compensate by throwing extra effort at the task. Keep that in mind for the next section.

Put those together and CBT's ask becomes strange: **when you're anxious, use the part of your brain that anxiety just turned down to talk yourself out of the anxiety.** It's like being told to read the manual for your flashlight while the power is out.

## And ADHD makes it worse

Here is where it gets personal. I have ADHD. ADHD is, to a large extent, a disorder of executive function: difficulty holding things in working memory, inhibiting impulses, switching tasks on purpose, starting things that aren't interesting. A big meta-analysis of 83 executive-function studies (Willcutt et al., 2005) found weaknesses in groups with ADHD on every EF task, strongest for response inhibition, vigilance, working memory and planning, with medium effect sizes (0.46 to 0.69). The honest caveat, in the authors' words: EF weaknesses are "neither necessary nor sufficient to cause all cases of ADHD," and fewer than half of (mostly child) ADHD samples show impairment on any single EF test.

So for someone with ADHD, the PFC is already the weak link on a calm day. Anxiety then turns it down further. Remember the compensation trick from Attentional Control Theory, where anxious people keep performing by spending extra effort? With ADHD, extra executive effort is exactly the thing in short supply. And the treatment on offer is: use your PFC harder.

When I put it that way, I honestly can't see how CBT, in its "argue with your thoughts" form, was ever going to work well for me. It doesn't make sense.

## What makes more sense to me

If the problem is that the top-down machinery is unreliable, the natural move is to use strategies that need less of it, or that train it.

**Mindfulness, Brewer-style.** Brewer frames anxiety as a habit loop: trigger, behavior (worry), reward (a feeling of control, or avoiding something). Worry gets reinforced because it feels like doing something. His approach isn't to argue with the worry. It's to (1) notice the loop, (2) pay curious attention to what the worry actually gives you, so your brain's reward system updates the value of the habit on its own, and (3) offer it a "bigger, better offer": curiosity, noticing the body sensations, breathing. Noticing "tightness in my chest, there it is" is a much smaller ask than building a counter-argument. It works *with* the reward-learning system, the one that's still running when the PFC is offline, instead of relying on the one that isn't. There's early data too: in a small randomized trial of his app for generalized anxiety (Roy et al., 2021, n=65), anxiety scores (GAD-7) dropped 67% vs 14% for usual care, and the analysis suggested the effect ran through mindfulness reducing worry. Small, self-reported, against usual care rather than CBT, and Brewer is an author, so I hold it loosely.

**Concentration practice.** If executive function is a muscle that's weak (ADHD) and gets weaker under load (anxiety), then directly training attention, with calm, boring reps, seems more sensible to me than trying to use it at its worst moment. Meditation as attention training is the closest thing I know to a gym for the PFC.

**Meds, probably.** If the PFC is under-powered, raising the floor directly also makes sense. In the big network meta-analysis of ADHD medications (Cortese et al., 2018), stimulants beat placebo in adults with standardized effects of about 0.8 (amphetamines) and 0.5 (methylphenidate) on clinician ratings, which is large for psychiatry (short-term data, around 12 weeks). I don't want to overclaim here, but "fix the hardware, then do the software" seems more coherent to me than "run harder software on hardware that's struggling."

## The strongest case against me

I want this post to be solid, so here is the best version of the other side I can build.

**1. CBT has a lot of evidence.** It's one of the most tested psychotherapies there is. A meta-analysis of 41 placebo-controlled trials for anxiety and related disorders (Carpenter et al., 2018, N=2,843) found CBT beats placebo on target symptoms with a moderate effect (Hedges' g = 0.56), and roughly triples the odds of responding. (It also leaves room for my view: across 87 studies, only about half of patients respond to CBT for anxiety; Loerinc et al., 2015.) Whatever I think of the mechanism, "it doesn't make sense" has to explain why it works in trials at all.

**2. CBT for adult ADHD exists and seems to help.** Safren et al. (2010, *JAMA*, n=86) found CBT beat an attention-matched control (relaxation plus education): 53% vs 23% responders on clinician global ratings, maintained at 12 months. But notice two things: every participant was *already on medication*, and the program was mostly skills (organizing and planning, managing distractibility, procrastination), with adaptive thinking as one module among several. So it's consistent with my view: raise the floor with meds, then teach concrete habits. And the Cochrane review of CBT for adult ADHD (Lopez et al., 2018) concludes there is only "low-quality evidence" that it may help in the short term.

**3. You don't do CBT during the panic.** This is the best rebuttal, I think. The point of CBT homework is to practice the reappraisal when you're *calm*, when the PFC *is* online, so that with enough repetition it becomes automatic and doesn't need much executive function later. That's how we learn any skill. Fair. But then the thing doing the work is repetition and habit formation, not reasoning in the moment. And if the active ingredient is practice that slowly changes your automatic responses, mindfulness gets you there with a much smaller executive-function bill per rep.

**4. A lot of what works in CBT for anxiety is exposure.** Exposure therapy (gradually facing the feared thing and letting the fear decrease) is behavioral, not argumentative. It's closer to extinction learning than to debate. Again, the strongest part of the package looks less like "think better thoughts" and more like "let your brain learn something new from experience."

**5. Mindfulness isn't magic either.** Against evidence-based treatments (often CBT), mindfulness-based programs come out at essentially zero difference (d ≈ 0.00; Goldberg et al., 2018). The same holds for ACT vs CBT (A-Tjak et al., 2015). The most impressive trial I know (Hoge et al., 2023, n=276) found an 8-week mindfulness course (MBSR) noninferior to escitalopram, a first-line drug, for anxiety disorders, with 8% dropping out for side effects on the drug vs none on MBSR. But that's a match for a drug, not a win over CBT. For adult ADHD, mindfulness-based cognitive therapy added to usual care helped (Janssen et al., 2019, d = 0.41), but a 2021 meta-analysis found only small effects against active controls (Oliva et al., 2021). The field also built hybrids, like MBCT, which trains people to step back from thoughts rather than dispute them. That suggests the camps are converging.

**6. The neuroscience is about acute stress, and the moderator data are mixed.** Arnsten's work is about what happens *during* stress. It doesn't directly test whether people with weaker executive function benefit less from CBT. The little direct evidence I found is in older adults and cuts both ways: in one pilot, anxious older adults whose executive function stayed low didn't respond to CBT, while those whose EF improved did (Mohlman & Gorman, 2005); in another, low cognitive flexibility didn't predict overall CBT outcome, but did predict getting less out of the cognitive-restructuring part specifically (Johnco et al., 2014). That last result is close to my claim. I couldn't find an adequately powered study in adults with ADHD and anxiety.

## Where I land

After steelmanning it, my claim is narrower but I believe it more:

> The idea that you can *reason your way out* of anxiety, using the brain region that anxiety impairs, doesn't make sense to me, and makes even less sense with ADHD. The parts of CBT that seem to work (exposure, skills, practice) look a lot like learning through experience and repetition. Mindfulness and concentration practice go straight for that, with a smaller executive-function bill, and meds can raise the floor.

Things I'd love to know (and would love to be corrected on):

- Does baseline executive function (or ADHD) predict *worse* CBT outcomes for anxiety in working-age adults? If CBT works equally well regardless, my argument is in trouble.
- Has anyone compared mindfulness-based treatment and CBT head-to-head in adults with ADHD *and* anxiety?
- In clinical practice, how much of "CBT" today is still cognitive restructuring vs. exposure, behavioral activation and mindfulness-style acceptance (ACT)?

*This is my personal view as someone who has thought about this a lot, not medical advice. If you're struggling, talk to a professional you trust.*
