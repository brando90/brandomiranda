After I wrote *Why CBT Doesn't Make Sense to Me*, I got a long comment from the strategic-therapy side of the field. It made five points. I am posting them roughly as they came, because they push on my argument from a direction I had not considered, and then I say what I plan to check.

## 1. The studies are full of confounds

Effect studies of CBT have many confounds, and the biggest is therapy time. People sit with someone for an hour a week and say what is bothering them. Venting and being heard help, whatever technique is used. So "CBT beats a waitlist" tells us less than it sounds like: part of the effect may be "talking to someone every week" and not the cognitive technique.

## 2. The attempted solution is the problem

The problem is often not the problem. It is what we do to try to solve it. The solution becomes the problem. Insomnia is the standard example. You think "I have to sleep, I'll feel awful tomorrow, I have so much to do", and you start trying to make sleep happen. It does not work. Sleep is like a black cat: the more you chase it, the more it runs away. If you stay still, as if you were not chasing it, it comes closer. Strategic therapy works by interrupting the attempted solution.

## 3. Reason does not change behavior, emotion does

We live in what could be called the perversion of reason: we are reasoned over and over, and reasoning does not do it. Everyone knows broccoli and chicken breast are healthier than the alternative, and people do not eat them. The reason is pleasure. Smoking works the same way. Graphic warning pictures answered the pleasure with fear, and people kept smoking. Same with a phobia: you know the plane almost never crashes, and the fear stays. In strategic therapy the treatment is made to measure for the problem, and it works with four emotions: horror, pleasure, fear and rage. The claim is that this can resolve long-standing problems faster than slow, painful therapy.

## 4. Over-control backfires

The comment added a personal note: trying to optimize and control one's own health, which was the attempted solution, made things worse, and taking care of every detail meant losing the big things. Dropping the control helped. This one is an anecdote, not a study, but it is the same pattern as point 2.

## 5. There is more, and it is easy to misread

This is a delicate topic, and it can be misread if it is not explained carefully, so the full version has to come in person. I will update this post when I have it.

## What I plan to check

These are claims, not findings. I am running them through the literature as a separate, more careful outline for a paper, and I will say plainly where the evidence is thin or goes against them. Points 1 and 2 have an obvious test (attention-controlled trials; the sleep-effort and paradoxical-intention studies). Point 3 is the one I expect to be hardest to defend as stated, since pictorial warnings on cigarette packs are a testable claim with real data behind it. And Judson Brewer's work on anxiety and habit loops is the closest thing I know to a peer-reviewed version of points 2 and 3, so I will read it as evidence and not only as inspiration.
